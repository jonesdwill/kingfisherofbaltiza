## 1. Background and Introduction

*Written by Lara Novak*

Our trip to Southeast Greenland first became an idea in 2024, after an impromptu crossing of the North Sea from Scotland to Norway, with Meg and Lara continuing up to Lofoten. We realised that we were eager to explore this concept of ‘sailing to climb’, particularly doing so without relying on third parties. Naturally, Southeast Greenland presented itself as an enticing goal.

The southeast coast of Greenland is very sparsely populated compared to the west coast, with about 2700 inhabitants mostly concentrated in the town of Tasiilaq. The town of Kulusuk (population 204) has an airport with flights coming in from Reykjavik and Nuuk. Graah Fjord is about 160 miles further south. The fjord remains largely unexplored due to complicated weather and ice conditions as well as its remoteness. However, it was visited by the Swiss Alpine Club women's expedition in August 2025, who sailed from Iceland on a charter vessel captained by Vicente Castro and successfully put up several first ascents.

In addition to putting up alpine-style first ascents, we had two main goals for this trip:

1.  Sailing ourselves from Scotland to Graah Fjord and back on Meg’s boat Kingfisher, e.g. without using a third party to facilitate the travel. This also involved a refit of the boat prior to departure.

2.  Gaining experience and knowledge on how to plan such a trip, as it was the first expedition undertaken by all members.

In achieving these goals, it was important to us to have as little negative impact on our surroundings as possible, both in relation to the natural environment and the Greenlandic population (see [Section 14](#impact)).

The following report contains an account of our trip to climb in Graah Fjord, detailing all our preparations, information about the area, and post-expedition reflections.

15 July: Arrival in Vestmannaeyjar in the afternoon. Trip to the hotbaths as our first port of call.

17 July: Departure for Reykjavik. Moderate/strong westerlies meant we were tacking with the engine on in choppy waters for approx. 10 hours to reach the western point of Reykjanes Peninsula. Then easy sailing through fog to reach Reykjavik harbour at 22:30.

19-23 July: Explored Reykjavik and completed some jobs/chores before leaving for Greenland. Last crew member, Gabi, arrived.

24 July: Set sail at 1:00 for Kulusuk. Light winds and good weather.

26 July: 100 miles from Greenland coast, forward gear stopped engaging. We adjusted the morse cable and managed to engage forward. Continued under engine towards Kulusuk.

27 July: Approach to Kulusuk: Clear, no wind, sparse bergs. Stationed lookout up the mast to look for uncharted rocks. GPS offset was minimal. A few hundred meters from the anchorage, the gearbox began to slip in gear. We anchored quickly.

28 July: Visited Pirhuk Mountain Guides cabin. Collected the two rifles that they had kindly let us order to their address (see [Section 4.4](#firearms)). Solution was to swap the reverse and forwards clutch cone, but this required a 36mm socket we did not have.

29 July: Target practice at a snowpatch 2km out of the town.

30 July: Took the gearbox to Kulusuk airport. Managed to reverse the cones with the help of Bendt (airport manager) and colleague Mugu (policeman, mechanic, maybe more). Purchased 2 litres of ATF (gearbox oil). Reattached the gearbox after dinner, not yet tested.

31 July: Meg and Charlie successfully motored Kingfisher around in circles in Kulusuk Bay - both forwards and reverse were engaging. Will and Lara went to explore climbing in the vicinity, doing two single pitch routes in an easterly bay. Bella and Gabi found some bouldering.

1 August: No weather window for heading south until the 3rd so we went climbing. Did two (potentially) new routes: Lara and Meg put up ‘The Mists of Avalon’ 110m HVS 5a, Charlie and Will put up ‘Clutch Cone Corner’, 110m E1 5b.

2 August: Morning visit to the private collection museum. Took an afternoon motor to see Apusiaajik Glacier, approx. 9km NNE of Kulusuk. Gearbox output seal had been damaged in our deconstructions, leaking ATF. Installed Tupperware drip tray.

3 August: Had tea aboard the Annabel J. with owners Andrew and Máire Wilkes. Set off for Graah Fjord at 20:00. Gearbox oil topped up every 4.5 hours of motoring, about 40ml each time.

4 August: Sunny and calm day. Sailing by lunchtime, downwind 3-4 kts.

5 August: Fog descended at 4:30. Passed through an ice band (1/10ths). Due to visibility decided not to enter Graah Havn as initially planned and continued up the fjord. Ten miles up fog cleared into brilliant sunshine. Paradise! Anchored in 30m with 100m scope out in the steeply shelving anchorage bowl. By evening, catabatics (force 4) were coming over the land. Sat anchor watch.

6 August: Catabatic gusts force 5/6. Anchor dragged 60m around 2:00. Wind died at 5:00. Went on a first scouting mission up the north branch of the fjord, mostly dry glacier. Reached the col in foggy conditions before heading back. Identified a scramble descent from a ridgetop that looked promising.

7 August: Headed up the southern valley to investigate a climb up to the ridge. Abandoned the attempt due to complicated descent and poor rock (see [Section 5.6](#climbing)). Scouted further up the valley and found promising southwest-facing slabs with better rock quality in their first half.

8 August: Early start to the slabs identified the day before. Approach approx. 2.5km. Meg and Lara climbed ‘Right-Hand Slab Wall’ 240m VS 4b, Charlie and Will climbed ‘Original Route’ 240m VS 4b. Parties reunited and decided to retreat due to rapidly decreasing rock quality. Down in four abseils.

9 August: Climbed on *Boat Buttress* directly north of the anchorage. Lara and Meg climbed ‘Septic Arthritis’ 550m E2 5c; Charlie and Will climbed ‘The Flying Teabag’ 500m E1 5c. Descent by the previously identified scree slope onto the glacier, approx. 2km up the valley.

10 August: Boat team excursion with Meg, Lara, Bella and Gabi up the northern valley to look at the glacier. Charlie and Will took the our dinghy (the Phoebe Rose) to the ‘Iceberg Zoo’ on the east side of the island.

11 August: Team attempt for the ridgetop above *Boat Buttress*. Scrambled up the scree slope used as a descent on 9 August and scrambled up the eastern edge. A cairn was found where the SAC team topped out their route ‘Imaqa’ at approx. 1000m asl. We continued along to the highest point on the ridge, recorded as 1520m. Descent via the same route.

12 August: Lara and Will climbed ‘Solar Eclipse’ 150m E1 5b on *William’s Step*, an east-facing wall left of a waterfall 3.3km up the south valley. Meg and Charlie … Grib files showed a window to return to Kulusuk on the 13th.

13 August: Departure at noon. Thick fog for 4 hours heading east through the ice band, clear by nightfall.

14 August: Orca sighting a few meters from the boat. Pleasant passage with light winds.

15 August: Arrived in Kulusuk by late morning. Returned the rifles to Pirhuk cabin. Did a few repair jobs (fixed rip in the genoa, replaced shackle on the mainsheet).

16 August: Last morning in Greenland! Weighed anchor at 8:00. Motored east for 20 hours into light headwinds with a large low pressure system to the south. Lara’s right ring finger began to look swollen and feel hot. She began taking amoxicillin in case of risk of infection.

17 August: Wind came round, pointed the course to Vestmannaeyjar. Noticeable increase in air and sea temperature. Accompanied by a pod of pilot whales!

19 August: Ran the fuel tank dry. We had 50 litres of diesel in jerry cans, enough for 24 hours of motoring. Began to be much more sparing with the engine usage, moving at speeds between 1 and 4 kts.

21 August: Saw a breaching humpback. Turned on the engine as we approached Vestamannaeyjar and tied up at 16:30. Lara went to the hospital to get her finger checked and was put on IV antibiotics for four days (still living on board). She was advised to fly home from Reykjavik for further testing in the UK.

22-24 August: We remained in Vestmannaeyjar as the weather looked poor for departure until the 25th. Did chores/repairs.

25 August: The Kingfisher said goodbye to Lara who was flying home later that day. Left Vestmannaeyjar marina at 4:30.

26-27 August: Left pre-dawn. Glassy waters. Lots of motoring. Harvest moon.

28-30 August: Strong northerlies came in, averaging some 7 knots for a while with a peak of 8.3kts. Rounded butt of Lewis at 5 kts. Arrived late at night, collapsing into Ullapool in a heap of smelly clothes.

## 4. Preparation & Research

### 4.1 Contacts

*Written by Lara Novak*

The southeast coast of Greenland is relatively remote. We found reports from three notable trips in the area that provided useful information regarding both sailing logistics and climbing information. Mike Libecki, who has been on several expeditions to Skjoldungen Fjord between 2002 and 2017, was very helpful with information on areas with good climbing potential in the Skjoldungen area. Matteo della Bordella, who was part of a 33-day expedition in 2024 kayaking and climbing, gave useful information regarding their permit application process and alternative climbing areas nearer to Tasiilaq.

In early 2025, we shifted our focus to the fjord north of Skjoldungen, Graah Fjord (see Section 4.1 above). At the time we had not found any reports of expeditions here. In December 2025, the article about the SAC women’s expedition, led by Naomie Bürki, came out. They provided us with information about the locations and quality of the climbs they had done. Their skipper, Vincente Castro, gave us descriptions of the two potential anchorages (Graah Havn at the head of the fjord and the second at the head of the fjord).

Matt and Helen Spencely, who run Pirhuk Mountain Guiding in Kulusuk, were exceptionally helpful both in the planning stages of the expedition and after our arrival. They provided information on local ice conditions and let us deliver our rifles to their address. They advised us on where to ask about the tools we needed to open the gearbox, and offered to tow Kingfisher to a safer anchorage should a major north-easterly storm arrive while we were still unable to move. Matt also talked us through his polar bear safety protocol and provided information on climbing areas near Kulusuk in case we were unable to regain mobility in time to continue south.

### 4.2 Permits

*Written by Lara Novak*

We experienced some difficulty when looking into the Greenlandic permitting requirements for our trip. In accordance with the guidelines given by the Greenlandic Government (Naalakkersuisut), we established that we needed to apply for a type A (tourism/logistics) permit ( [<u>https://expeditionsgreenland.gl/en/apply-for-an-expedition-permit/tourism-logistics-expeditions-a</u>](https://expeditionsgreenland.gl/en/apply-for-an-expedition-permit/tourism-logistics-expeditions-a)), at least 12 weeks prior to departure. We needed an insurance provider that would cover up to DKK 1000000 for expedition SAR operations, as well as DKK 280000 for individual evacuations. The first stage of this process is paying a non-refundable application fee of DKK 4000 (£463). It then became clear that we needed a provider prepared to sign an insurance statement ([<u>https://expeditionsgreenland.gl/en/templates/insurance-statement</u>](https://expeditionsgreenland.gl/en/templates/insurance-statement)) declaring that they are willing to pay out the above sums. It proved difficult to find an affordable insurance provider that would be willing to sign this statement.

As a result, we reconsidered the nature of our proposed climbing objectives. Our original plan had allowed for a temporary base camp further up the valley, from which we could attempt objectives a considerable distance from the boat. However, as we were unable to find an insurance provider willing to cover this, we instead focused on objectives that would keep us on land for no more than 24 hours at a time, meaning no permit was required. Unfortunately, we had already paid the non-refundable application fee by this time.

It is important to note that the rules and regulations are currently being readjusted (several of the pages on the Greenlandic Government (Naalakkersuisut) permit website are under construction) and that this information should not necessarily be considered applicable for future expeditions.

### 4.3 Firearms

*Written by Lara Novak*

We were advised to buy rifles rather than rent them, as the rentals available in Tasiilaq have been known to be dodgy at times, as well as being much heavier and generally harder to use. It seemed that if we were actually going to need the guns it would be best to have fully reliable, easy-to-use ones, especially given the general lack of gun knowledge in the expedition team.

We bought two Tikka T3x 30.06 rifles from the marine broker Orsiivik ApS in Nuuk. This was done very easily over email at the beginning of July to allow ample time for them to be shipped to Kulusuk. Buying the rifles was significantly more expensive than renting them, but we had arranged with Matt to sell them to him for half price on our return journey, making our total cost slightly over that of renting two rifles. Each gun cost 8499-kr (£975), and the shipping for both cost 1050-kr (£120).

Matt very kindly gave us 8 soft-point bullets. It is advised to use soft-point bullets for polar bear defence because their copper jackets hold them together, allowing them to penetrate deeper before expanding. They are therefore more effective in killing a large animal like a polar bear with fewer shots, which is preferable both for the safety of the person being attacked and for ensuring that the bear’s death is as humane as possible. We purchased 20 full-metal jacket bullets from the store in Kulusuk to use for target practice and as spares.

### 4.4 Boat Refit

*Written by Meg Loftus*

Preparing a 50 year-old wooden boat for her first trip to the Arctic is quite a serious undertaking. We had left an expansive 6 weeks to achieve this, and in hindsight we would probably recommend double or even triple this. Our saving grace in our great naivety was Johnson and Loftus Boatbuilders, of which one half is Meg’s father, and their levels of panicked action that maybe only familial ties can induce. Additionally, the crew of the Kingfisher worked hard at sanding, scraping, painting, cleaning, sawing, drilling, screwing and varnishing, with varying levels of success. At the very least we left the boatyard with more practical skills than we started with.

<p>

<em>Kingfisher Going Back in the Water.</em>

The jobs required included:

- Removing her Beta 43 engine, replacing the engine feet, mounting brackets and fuel injector pipes as well as repainting all over.

- Removing and cleaning the fuel tank which had suffered from ‘diesel bug’ for several years.

- Replacing the plywood boards beneath the fuel tank and domestic batteries.

- Remaking the rope locker hatch, this was done in plywood because we are not very good boatbuilders, but Will did paint a very pretty kingfisher on it.

- Removing the water tanks, cleaning and welding patches onto them as they had been leaking.

- Removing and inspecting the keelbolts, which were in good shape, and the arm bolts (between the floors and the frames) which were in bad shape. Replacing the arm bolts.

- Adding extra fastenings between the floors and the wood keel and along the garboard planking.

- Installing a manual pump to act as both sink drain and backup bilge pump.

- Adding running backstays to straighten the mast.

- Repainting the hull, cabinsides and deck, varnishing the hatch and doraid boxes, oiling the toerails and trim.

- Installing various wiring systems, including the new AIS system planned by Will and Charlie (see section …), an external satellite phone antenna, new engine and domestic battery banks, extra charging ports and replacing a damaged navigation light.

- Various assorted deck fittings including new sturdier stanchion bases for the safety rails, a jammer for the bobstay tensioning line and lashing points for the liferaft and dinghy.

- Cleaning the bilge - not to be laughed at!

- Sewing lee-cloths and cushion covers to facilitate sleeping in the cabin berths.

Of these jobs, Tim and Dan did the engine, water tanks, bolts and fastenings. They provided extensive advice and assistance in the other jobs but most of the grunt work was done by the Kingfisher crew.

<figure>

<img loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="" src="/trip-reports/greenland/media/kingfisher-prep-engine.webp" />
<p>

<em>Engine Going Back in Post-Overhaul.</em>

In addition to all of the actual work being done, Kingfisher needed a lot of new things. The main ones are outlined below.

- Liferaft: We went for a more expensive 24 hours+ liferaft, which is something to bear in mind when arctic sailing - 24 hour liferafts only contain supplies to last 1 day and must be supplemented with a grab bag.

- Batteries: These are used for starting the engine as well as domestic usage, so it is important they work. Cold reduces their efficiency as well as making engine starts harder, so worthwhile getting something newish.

- Outboard engine for the dinghy

- Horseshoe buoys with lights and rope.

- Anchor chain: Anchorages are often very deep in Greenland. We went for 70m of 8mm grade 70 chain, with 30m of rode. On top of this we carried spare rode which we used in Graah Fjord.

- Mainsail and genoa

- Secondary bilge pump

By the 8th of July it had become apparent that to leave on our projected date (the 10th) would mean tacking up the Minch for two days against strengthening northerlies; we therefore decided to get two days of packing, cleaning, and last-minute jobs done in one, and leave on the evening of the 9th. ### 5.3 Leg 2: Sailing to Greenland

*Written by Lara Novak*

After an initial 24-hour bout of seasickness all crew members felt well. The next few days were peaceful, straightforward and dry. All was going well until, on the 26th, I was awakened from my slumber by the sound of engine distress. Apparently forward gear wasn't engaging. Meg descended into the engine bay to inspect while I turned the boat around to face toward Iceland again - it is not a great idea to head towards a potential iceband without engine power. After a few hours of fiddling around, forward gear seemed to be engaging again and we decided to continue on to Kulusuk to troubleshoot further. Whilst nervous, we all would have been a bit devastated to get so close but not quite make it to Greenland.

### 5.4 Kulusuk

*Written by Lara Novak*

On the morning of the 28th July, we went over to the Pirhuk cabin, where the two guns were delivered. One of the guides there showed Gabi, Charlie, Will and I a large snowpatch 2km away that we could use for shooting practice.

When we returned to the boat the gearbox was on deck, and it was now evident that the forward gear cone had worn enough that the gear no longer engaged. One solution was to open the gearbox and reverse the cones to gain at least forward gear again, but this required a 36mm socket that we did not have. In the meantime, we started looking into ordering a replacement gearbox in case fixing ours proved impossible. This was not without its complications, as the supplier in Iceland was out of stock, so it would have to first be ordered to them, then transported to Kelfavik airport and shipped via Icelandair Cargo.

On the 29th we went back to Pirhuk and were very warmly received with coffee and fresh bread, as well as the helpful advice to ask at the airport for the tools we needed. With the gearbox safely in a bucket, we set off. Walking into the single-room airport terminal none too inconspicuously, we asked to speak to the airport manager, Bendt. At first slightly taken aback by the six stinky strangers asking for help, he proved to be very friendly. His colleague Mugu took us to the workshop, where the gearbox was opened within seconds by a 36mm socket and a powerful impact driver. With the cones successfully reversed, our situation was looking up. Back on board after dinner, we sealed the gearbox back together and Meg crawled into the engine bay to reattach it, to be tested the following day.

Meg and Lara had once again walked up the hill much quicker than Charlie and I, and for the second time the \*king line\* up the buttress had been stolen from under our noses. The second pitch took quite some working out - Charlie climbed left along a ledge for some distance before encountering bad rock and reversing. After the luxurious 10 minute approach, we began up the center of the buttress. The first 50m pitch had excellent climbing, with steep initial moves to get to the first bits of gear. The climbing then became more amenable if much bolder. We encountered a rather large loose block on pitch two that Meg tentatively scaled before performing some delicate moves on exposed ground to a hanging belay. These pitches comprised the steepest and most sustained climbing of the route, all at about HVS/E1. We ate some lunch, peacefully looking down at a very small Kingfisher in the bay below and enjoying watching Charlie and Will scaling the steep wall to our right.

The next pitch was once again excellent, up a set of right-leaning parallel finger cracks. However, the ground above looked difficult, with the crack system petering out rather ominously. I therefore embarked on a serpentine two pitches, starting with a 20m traverse to the right before heading back left and up another crack system. Meg did battle with a rather steep and dirty corner (the hardest pitch, E2) to arrive at a section of lower-angled rock. At this point, it was 17:00 and the boys radioed us that they had reached the top. This gave us a rather false sense of hope that we, too, were nearing the end, which was not to be true. However, we were having an excellent time in the warm Greenlandic sun!

We continued to make our way up the face, which had begun to include some choss scrambling - but with enough quality climbing to keep the route enjoyable. The boys radioed again that they were back on shore at 18:00. We continued upwards on our seemingly never-ending climb. After 12 pitches, we reached another large ledge with a distinctive boulder Will and Charlie had mentioned as being at the top of their route as well. The ground became much less steep, so we unroped and scrambled another few hundred meters to the top of the buttress, reaching it at about 22:00.

We skirted around the east edge of the ridge over large boulders and descended the scree down to the glacier, staying high and traversing northeast to avoid the cliff drop-off to our right. We reached the boat at 23:30, very grateful for the warmth, comfort and delicious meal awaiting us.

*Pimple Mountain*

*Written by Meg Loftus*

After our failure on Phoebe Rose Ridge, Charlie and I were certain we wanted to reach the summit of something, so we picked the easiest thing we could see. To the north of the bay was a smaller summit, in fact barely more than a blemish on the rugged skyline, bearing all possible resemblance to a cresting zit on an adolescent face. Thus it developed the rather unfortunate name of Pimple Mountain. We set off early in thick fog. I thought I knew where I was but it turned out I didn’t, thankfully Charlie led us the correct direction and the fog soon lifted as we scrambled up grass and low angled slabs. We were filled with anticipation as we reached the shoulder and headed up some steep snow slopes. A few hundred metres of scrambling would lead us to a lovely loose ridge soaring away to the summit. We gained this easily and with great excitement. The north side of pimple mountain fell away abruptly into the northern branch of Graah Fjord, a dusky frightening blue and littered with icebergs. For Charlie and I who are both quite obsessed with finding new anchorages, mountains and places in general it was tremendous to see a whole new part of the landscape. We had hoped (if we had more time) to sail round into that branch and attempt Charlie’s dream mountain Rigspidsen, but from our observations the northern branch of the fjord would be more serious to sail into and without an anchorage safe enough to leave Kingfisher. Ridspidsen is a shapely peak as a mountaineering objective but we didn’t think there would be much rock climbing on this side. It is heavily glaciated and would involve several complex crossings.

### 5.8 Leg 5: Sailing to Iceland

*Written by Lara Novak*

Our last morning in Greenland, the 16th of August, was greeted reluctantly by all crew members as we dragged ourselves out of bed to weight anchor. To our great joy, the engine engaged and we said goodbye to Kulusuk. We motored east for the first 20 hours into light headwinds, with the knowledge of a large low-pressure system to the south which would bring a northeasterly shift. When this arrived we began sailing, and after 24 hours, both the air and sea temperature noticeably increased as we exited the East Greenland current. Over the next two days, wind remained light but continued to come round to the north. Two of my fingers on my right hand had become alarmingly swollen and painful after leaving Kulusuk and I began to take co-amoxiclav in case this proved to be an infection.

In the early hours of the 19th, the wind died and we attempted to start the engine, which made an alarming noise. At first fearful of some more technical issue, we realised we had run the fuel tank dry; the last time we had refilled had been in Kulusuk on the 2nd of August before heading to Graah Fjord. We still had 50 litres of diesel in jerry cans, enough for about 24 hours of motoring, but it was essential to not squander this as it would be needed to enter Vestmannaeyjar. We thus became a lot more frugal with our engine usage!

### 6.2 Slab Crag

*GPS Coordinates: 63°32'30.76"N 41°33'22.86"W*

*Slab Crag* sits halfway up the southern valley, a ~400-500m wall of waterwashed slabs. Rock quality is good to hald height (250m), where it turns.

*SW Facing*

*Approach:* Head west from the anchorage, staying north of the river. The approach picks its way along the riverbank through sections of large boulders until the slabs become visible, characterised by crack / chimney systems on either side leading to a large roof system at half height (250m).

*Descent:* Fixed line of 3x50m 1x60m abseils from half-way. A walk-off descent may be possible by traversing around to the *Boat Buttress* descent but has not been scouted.

#### 6.2.1 Original Route, 240m \* VS 4b

*6 Pitches, 240m. FA: Will Jones & Charlie Banford. 8 Aug 2026.*

Starting at the RH crack system, the route trends diagonally left TO join the LH crack system and finishes at a large gully above roofs. A direct start up the LH crack could be made if snow patch were smaller.

1\. 40m Climb corner and cracks just left of RHS chimney crack system. Belay on dirty ledge on the left by rightwards facing flake.

2\. 30m Straight up slabs from flake onto another large ledge with loose rock. Walk left to belay at base of 10m corner.

3\. 35m Up corner and left to belay in LHS chimney crack system.

4\. 50m Follow crack system to belay ledge edge on right.

5\. 50m Continue up cracks to belay on grassy ledge at the base of blank slabs below overhangs.

6\. 4b 40m Boldly up slabs to left edge of roofs. Into gully above roofs and immediately back left into larger gully to belay.

#### 6.2.2 Right Hand Slab Route, 240m \* VS 4b

*6 Pitches, 240m. FA: Meg Loftus & Lara Novak 8 Aug 2026.*

An enjoyable slab route beginning up a wide crack.

1\. 40m HS Climb the wide, easy-angled crack.

2\. 40m HS Continue up the crack.

3\. 40m HS Continue up, staying left of a section of loose flakes before reentering the crack system to belay.

4\. 40m HS Move up and stay in the left crack at the fork. Come over a lip and continue up the crack to belay under a large loose roof.

5\. 40m VS Move boldly diagonally left across the blank slab to gain a long ledge. Traverse this to the left for 10m to place gear. Continue left to belay under a large block on the arete.

6\. 30m VS Step out left around the corner and climb up the steeper wall to a large belay ledge below a loose ramp.

### 6.3 Boat Buttress

*GPS Coordinates: 63°32'16.65"N 41°31'13.26"W*

A granite buttress overlooking the anchorage at the head of the fjord with reasonable rock quality if a little vegetated.

*E Facing*

#### 6.3.1 Septic Arthritis, 550m \*\* E2 5c.

*550m. FA: M Loftus & L Novak. 9 Aug 2026.*

A good route, with a mixture of pure climbing pitches and some scrambling over chossy ledges.

1\. 50m E1 5b Start with a bold move off the ground, starting at the lowest point of the buttress face. The climbing then becomes less hard but bolder.

2\. 30m HVS 4c Surmount a large block to the right. From here step left and up over crumbly rock to belay below a slabbier section.

3\. 25m HVS 4c Delicate moves over the slab to a grassy topout onto a large ledge. Belay at the huge boulder in the centre of the ledge.

4\. 30m VS 4c Leaving the boulder to your right, climb the right-leaning twin finger cracks on the wall behind.

5\. 20m Traverse right across grass ledges to the base of a crack.

6\. 25m VS 4c Move 3 meters up the crack before traversing left to another crack system. Follow this to the base of a dirty corner.

7\. 25m E2 5b Balance on small sandy edges to dig out some cam placements before stepping right onto a blank bulge. Move upwards 2 meters and step back left onto easier ground. Care needed as it is mostly scree.

The next 5 pitches are a case of finding the best way to the top, with a mixture of scrambling/scree and sections of enjoyable climbing. After reaching another large ledge with a huge boulder, an easy scramble for another 300m takes you to the top of the buttress.

#### 6.3.2 The Flying Teabag, 500m \*\* E1 5c

*6+ Pitches, 500m. FA: W Jones & C Banford. 9 Aug 2026.*

Start up the obvious large face on the RHS of the buttress.

1\. 50m 5a Head diagonally rightwards to climb the LHS of the arête on generally good rock to a ledge on the right at 1/3 height.

2\. 45m 5c Continue up the arête on more good rock and into cracks on the face. Up face (crux) to belay at large blocks at 2/3 height.

3\. 60m Traverse rightwards past large chockstones onto broken ground. Continue diagonally rightwards up heathery ledges until the base of a chimney.

4\. 50m Climb chimney onto more broken terrain, and on until the rock becomes solid again.

5 & 6. 100m Continue up easy but solid ground to large ledge systems.

Scramble up ledge systems for another 300m until a clear flattening at top of buttress.

## 7. Medical Report

*Written by Lara Novak*

### 7.1 Insurance

As mentioned in [Section 4.2](#permits), we initially had some difficulty in securing an insurance provider for our activities on land. As Graah Fjord is incredibly remote, having coverage for SAR (search and rescue) procedures was most important. Many SAR insurance providers that advertise worldwide coverage exclude a small selection of places, often including Greenland. We eventually signed on to the WTW Montagne insurance available through the FFCAM (Federation Francais des Clubs Alpins et de Montagne). To do this, we all signed up as members of the Club Alpin Francais Corse du Sud to become FFCAM licensed members. This comes with a personal and emergency insurance costing for those over 24 years old and for those younger. We each purchased the Worldwide extension for an additional pp, which is required if the activities to be covered are taking place outside of Europe/Morocco.

We purchased a ‘Medical Only’ single-trip policy from Topsail for the duration 22 July to 26 August, e.g. covering time on board between departure from Iceland for Greenland up to arriving back in Iceland on the way home. This cover is applicable to medical and repatriation emergencies whilst at sea. The total cost for one month was £750.33.

### 7.2 First Aid Training and Competency

Whilst several expedition members have done first aid courses over the years and have some basic knowledge of emergency first response, it was decided that for such a trip a more extensive training and knowledge base would be crucial. This is due not only to the highly remote nature of Graah Fjord in case of a severe trauma injury, but also due to the extended length of our trip in adverse and isolated conditions (six weeks on board, more often than not out of immediate reach of professional medical care and hospitalisation). This added a higher risk of serious illnesses/conditions arising, which is a very large area of knowledge not addressed in standard first aid courses.

For this reason, Lara attended a four-day advanced medicine Wilderness Medical Training (WMT) course in Kendal, run by Barry Roberts, from the 6th to the 9th of June. The course covers injury and illness, as well as:

- Use of prescription only medicines (POMs).

- Invasive training (wound stapling, intramuscular injections)

- Advanced fractures

- Advanced dislocations

It is designed for laypeople travelling in remote environments to build confidence in using advanced techniques when professional emergency help is unavailable. The certification also comes with a 2-year accreditation to purchase POMs, with the condition that these will only be administered by the trained person and in the case where professional help is not available (see [Section 7.3](#fa-kit)).

Provisioning for this expedition had several aspects of interest. Firstly, the fact that restocking after departure should be kept to a minimum. We wanted to avoid restocking in Iceland where groceries are notoriously expensive, and in Kulusuk fresh food is delivered only once a week and it would be poor form to buy up all the fresh produce.

We therefore needed to stock enough food for 6 weeks, for 6 people. We have heard of past trips where provisioning was not adequate; tensions tend to run high and tempers become short. Being stuck and hungry in a single room with 6 people could lead to mutiny if we weren't careful…

To ensure enough food was brought, Lara created an excel of ‘example meals’ with individual quantities of each component, to then be multiplied by the number of crew and amount of times the meal would be eaten. This is potentially overkill, however it did reveal some astounding numbers that would probably have been vastly underestimated if going off of a ‘gut feel’.

It should be noted that space was less of a problem for us than on other climbing expeditions; having a boat allowed us to take many not necessarily space-efficient ingredients.

Another consideration was gas. Some simple maths showed that we would want at least 10kg of gas between leaving and returning to Iceland (approx. 4 weeks). We therefore took 1 6kg Calor propane gas bottle, 1 3.9kg Calor propane gas bottle, and 1 5kg Icelandic bottle that we would be able to exchange in Iceland for the final leg if necessary (which it was). This amount of gas was calculated to include copious tea drinking whilst on watch and to facilitate the creation of elaborate, gas-intensive meals.

Some observations:

- Milk powder: We found full-fat milk powder to be incredibly useful, and used all 4kg.

- Egg powder: Its sometimes questionable smell and consistency were offputting to several crew members.

- For all perishables, it is best to buy in smaller units (e.g. 200g blocks of cheese instead of 800g, small jars of pesto) to minimise waste in the event of not eating it fast enough.

- 1 x VHF radio on Kingfisher for regular boat comms.

- 1 x handheld VHF radio for land party to communicate with Kingfisher.

- 1 x Iridium 9555 satellite phone and Iridium GTC sim, initially loaded with a 100 minute phone plan. To be used for remote phone calls in case of emergency and to get weather via a data modem. An antenna was installed on the pushpit to boost signal and enable cabin usage.

- 2 x Garmin InReach Mini 2s, one with Garmin’s Unlimited plan and one with their Essential plan (50 texts per month). This would give flexibility to update family and friends, and reliably track our position.

- 4 x two-way radios, to communicate between the 4 climbers.

We had intended for each climbing team to have an InReach which could be used to text the SAT phone on Kingfisher (leaving the handheld VHF in a shore barrel). However, the Iridium network lagged significantly, especially when cloudy. Texts took up to 20 minutes to send. The least reliable connection was Garmin to SAT phone. We had tested successfully before getting to Graah Fjord, but the devices must have been piggybacking off other mobile networks. On the other hand, the range of the VHF and two-way radios proved good enough for what we were doing.

Communication between climbing parties was therefore primarily by two-way radio, with one climbing party taking the unlimited Garmin and the other climbing party take the handheld VHF. The secondary Garmin was either left with Kingfisher or taken by the second climbing team depending on the situation. Using this method, the climbers and Kingfisher were able to communicate reliably from a few kilometres away. It should be noted that if we had pursued objectives with a poorer line-of-sight, these methods may not have been sufficient.

Overall, the second Garmin found very little use and was unnecessary other than for redundancy. A second handheld VHF may have been preferred, at half the cost.

Regarding the SAT phone, by the time we got to Reykjavik we had ended up using some 40 minutes of phone plan, primarily from testing our weather system before we left. We therefore purchased an extra 100 minutes on top of the original 100 minutes. This decision proved useful, as we ended up making several calls for advice on our mechanical (gearbox) issues.

It seems important to mention that Kulusuk has a 4G mast and we were able to get mobile data eSIMs. The eSIMs did not allow cheap calls and texts but the data was useful for troubleshooting our gearbox problems.

### 10.2 Emergency Devices

Both Garmin InReach devices have an inbuilt SOS function that works with any active (or suspended) subscription. These work on relatively low power output (~1-2W) Iridium L-band (1.6 GHz) radio signals to the commercial Iridium network. The SAT phone works on the same network, frequency and wattage, with the added advantage of calling and access to an external antenna. Though, it is important to note that the antenna doesn’t boost signal, rather direct the signal (like putting a reflector around a bulb), so with lots of cloud cover these options are less attractive.

Kingfisher has a marine grade EPIRB on board and Will owns a PLB. The EPIRB floats, has a better battery, and will automatically set off in a depth of 2-4m water. The PLB is manual. The disadvantage to both is that there is no handshake back, whereas an Iridium device can receive response.

Kingfisher’s standard marine VHF works on ~156–162 MHz channels, with a distress button linked to two dedicated AIS frequencies (161.975 & 162.025 MHz) for rescue. These work entirely on line of sight, and with a whip on Kingfisher’s main mast (~8m) the range of Kingfisher’s VHF is about 20km. In Graah Fjord this was irrelevant, but was relevant for all the other coastal sailing.

### 10.3 Charts

Kingfisher has a Garmin GPS 451 installed in the cabin onto which we could load charts of the UK and Iceland. We also took paper charts of the UK, Iceland and Greenland. However, the paper and published online charts of Greenland leave much to be desired - the best charts are still those by Gino Watkin’s air survey in 1931! We instead made charts of Graah Fjord using QGIS and traced out rocks using satellite imagery.

To load charts onto the Garmin, we would have to buy a special SD card which are expensive and not user-friendly. We also needed something to see the AIS on. The best solution we found was to buy a cheap second hand tablet and use a combination of Navionics charts and OpenCPN, an open-source chart plotter for our custom charts. We also had a linux laptop with OpenCPN as a backup.

### 10.4 AIS (Automatic Identification System)

Kingfisher had no AIS before the trip, but she does have a VHF whip on the main mast (a range which includes AIS frequencies). AIS is used to communicate information (speed, size, etc.) between nearby ships, which is particularly useful in busy ports and coastal sailing. Since AIS transponders are expensive and we are a small private vessel we decided to only install a receiver.

### 10.5 Weather & Ice Forecasts

It would seem that getting reliable weather information at sea has been solved if you buy an expensive Iridium GO!, or give in to Elon and buy a Starlink, which you then have to power (and is not allowed in Greenland).

We brought a Linux laptop with us to do all the handiwork (which is chargeable by 12V, though quicker with an inverter). We had heard Windows has a habit of auto-updating and using SAT phone minutes for background tasks, which we wanted to avoid.

At first we tried setting up firewalls on the Linux system so that only e-mail traffic goes in and out. We could also control the data call from the laptop, and do all the DNS handshaking business that goes on with e-mails. However, most e-mail client’s IPs change dynamically over time, so once away from land we couldn’t use the cached IPs and we were having problems with the DNS lookups going stale. We could have fixed this with a static IP but this is a fair bit more time and work. Our solution, as suggested by Tim Loftus and Alasdair Flint, was to use the SAT phone as a data modem paired with the provider UUPlus to email Saildocs, an open-source weather service that sends back requested GRIB files. UUPlus costs \$35/month and allowed us to get decent resolution GFS/ECMWF/ICON forecasts for more than a week out.

The data phone line on the Iridium 9555 SAT phone allows for ~2KB/s data transfer, which is sufficient for the ~20KBs GRIB files we were fetching. The SAT phone plugs into a coax cable running to an external antenna on the pushpit, and also the laptop via USB. We found its connection stability to be variable, dropping significantly on cloudy days.

To fetch ice forecasts, we set up a relay on a GitHub server that finds the latest forecast, compresses the PDF (to below 50KBs) and pings it to a mailbox. This is somewhat overcomplicated; however, ice flow ended up being minimal for us and it was sufficient to check the forecasts with data in Iceland and Kulusuk.

## 11. Graah Fjord Anchorage and Charting

*Written by Charlie Banford*

<a id="anchorage"></a>

Graahs Fjord was not accurately surveyed or charted, with the best available maps featuring clear errors when compared to the high-resolution satellite images which are available. Shallow rocks could be seen through the water and shoal areas also could be seen. However this technique would of course miss many hazards, it was best used as a general guidance hopefully avoiding some dangers but did not guarantee safe passage. A sharp watch for rocks was kept at all times in the fjord.

The software QGIS was used to draw charts based off the satellite images, these could then be viewed using open chart plotter on a tablet. These charts proved their worth when they allowed us to safely navigate into the fjord in poor visibility.

We took time to record soundings on the approach to and around the anchorage. This was done using kingfishers depth sounder in deeper areas and a lead line from the dingy for the shallow areas of the anchorage. Location data was recorded for the sounding using the GPS on the inreach. These data points were then added to the charts once back home. It is hoped these charts will allow for safer navigation into the fjord for future sailors.

Areas suspected to be hazardous are labeled on the map in red. With the passage to the east of the island thought to be more shallow based on observations from the dinghy, it also collects a significant quantities of grounded icebergs. This likely is why the anchorage at the head of the fjord was largely ice free. Passage to the west of the island through the narrower but seemingly cleaner and deeper channel is better.

The shore shoals steeply around the anchorage which presented problems when finding a spot shallow enough to anchor without a risk of grounding on the shelf. We anchored in 30m near the south east corner of the anchorage. Holding was found to be good in clayey mud. Other boats appear to have anchored towards the north west side of the anchorage.

The winds in the anchorage were found to be strongly katabatic (to be expected given the surrounding terrain) with winds forecast as force 4 from the north west causing us to frequently sail directly over our anchor as they reversed direction in strong gusts. It would be wise to be ready to seek shelter in Graahs Haven if any strong winds are forecast.

## 12. Weather and Ice Conditions

*Written by Meg Loftus*

### Timings

In comparison with other Arctic sailing expeditions, ours ran on a very tight time schedule. This was done to avoid difficult ice concentrations found along the Greenlandic coast early in the season, as well as the unpredictable stormy weather at the season’s end. However, it did mean that we had only 2 weeks of climbing planned in our original schedule, and when we ran into engine difficulties we had to cut that down to 8 days. We would recommend leaving earlier in the year rather than staying later, as in recent years the coastal ice has cleared by the start of July, and indeed we met another small yacht when we first arrived in Iceland who had already spent several weeks cruising and were on their way home! We would probably have chosen to leave earlier if it were not for the tight turnaround between Meg and Charlie’s final exams and the extensive refit.

### Ice

We saw no ice until within 60 miles of the coast approaching Kulusuk, and even then only a few big bergs. Despite this we had our only ice-related anchoring incident that very first evening, when a bergy bit about ½ size of Kingfisher had to be pushed away with poles and the dinghy. Heading down towards Graah Fjord we were generally more than 50 miles offshore and saw only a few bergs. There was a thicker band of bergs and brash from 5 miles offshore to 20 miles offshore around the entrance to the fjord, most likely due to the ice-producing glaciers in the neighbouring \[\] fjord.

Graah Fjord has no tidewater glaciers; icebergs blown in from offshore collected and ground on the northeast side of the island next to our anchorage, which we affectionately referred to as the ‘Iceberg Zoo’. We had no incidents with bergs or growlers at out anchorage at the head of the fjord.

### Weather

We were very lucky with the weather and we would happily admit that the trip could have been a lot more difficult, serious and most certainly damp had we not been so blessed. Most of our passages were warm with light winds between 3 and 4. We did a lot of motoring and saw very little rain. This is generally common in Greenlandic coastal waters but was unusual during our North Atlantic passages, especially on the rain front - with detailed modern forecasting it is generally possible to make 5-6 day passages with no unexpected strong wind, but to avoid being rained on too is very lucky! In Greenland we saw classic Arctic weather, with about 65% of days being clear, sunny and calm. When we arrived in Kulusuk they were just coming to the end of a heatwave and it was very warm, with the crew sunbathing on the deck and going swimming. 30% saw thick mist, which generally burnt off slowly as the day went on, receding from the land first and down the fjords towards the sea. In Graah Fjord we saw some impressive cloud inversions. The remaining 5% were misty and rainy. During our time anchored in Kulusuk we saw winds up to force 4, but during the planning of our return journey we saw winds of force 6 in the forecast in which the Kulusuk anchorage is likely to be unsafe. In Graah fjord we had one night (of 8) in which we experienced winds that were forecast force 4 over the land, but were strongly catabatic with gusts up to force 5 / 6. After that we saw no winds stronger than force 2 / 3. Generally there would be a light breeze from the sea during the day, with the nights being still or with gentle breezes from the land. The day breeze would begin around noon when the morning fog had receded.

## 13. Polar Bear Protocol

*Written by Lara Novak*

### Flares

Pencil flares are recommended to carry for polar bear defence, but we were unable to purchase them in the UK nor in Iceland (they are classified as hazardous explosives and therefore cannot be shipped). We therefore each carried a red handheld flare with us at all times when on land; these would have the same desired effect, but are slightly more cumbersome to carry and deploy than pencil flares.

### Rifles

When the climbing teams went to shore they carried one rifle between the four of them to the base of the climbing for that day, and returned to pick it up upon descending. This provided a good level of safety on most days (where the start and end point of the route were in the same place) other than on the 9th of August when both teams climbed up *Boat Buttress* and descended down onto the glacier, leaving about an hour and a half of travel down the valley without a rifle. However, short of climbing with the rifle strapped to your back, this is an unavoidable level of objective danger that we had accepted. We would load the magazine with soft-point bullets before leaving the boat but have the chamber empty. A Ziploc of spare bullets was kept in the bottom of the gun bag for quick access if necessary.

The boat team kept the second rifle, as they were going to shore to explore during the day, and it is not unheard of for bears to climb onto boats. Both rifles would be unloaded and cleaned upon everyone returning to the boat for the night.

### Air horns

We had purchased two handheld airhorns for the trip as another deterrent method, as loud noises are recommended to scare off curious bears. There is speculation that noises too similar to that of ice breaking, such as warning shots, are less effective as bears are used to it; our theory was that an air horn, being quite a foreign sound, might work better. Luckily we did not have to test this theory.

The discussed protocol upon encountering a bear was: Stand together and keep silent. If the bear moves on, make your way back to the boat whilst remaining vigilant. If the bear shows signs of irritation or aggression and begins to approach, use the flares (or air horns) to try and deter it. If the bear charges, shoot when it is 30 meters away. DON’T throw a flare past the bear as this could make it charge towards you.

## 14. Impact Assessment

*Written by Lara Novak*

<a id="impact"></a>

### Waste

To minimise our impact on the community in Kulusuk, we stored all waste (both dry and food) on board until returning to Iceland. We did not restock on fresh fruit and vegetables until returning to Iceland so as not to deplete the local supply that is shipped in once a week.

### Carbon footprint

It was intended that no flights would be taken during the expedition; unfortunately, Lara had to fly out of Reykjavik on the return journey after being admitted to the hospital in Vestmannaeyjar due to suspected septic arthritis.

As a sailing vessel we aimed to be moving under sail as often as was feasible. However, the nature of such a trip (with restricted weather and time windows), paired with the characteristic light winds of the Arctic summer, meant we spent a fair amount of time under engine. We had estimated 500 litres of fuel usage before leaving; we ended up using about 600 litres.

Our electricity consumption was made up of the cabin lights, the navigation lights, charging phones/comms devices, and the engine ignition. The domestic side of this is usually covered by the 100W solar panel we have on board, and any extra power is supplied from the engine. We did not have to run the engine when stationary for charging purposes.

Kingfisher has a small liquid fuel heater run on diesel, which it is only possible to have lit when stationary. We had estimated 7 litres of diesel usage for heating in anticipation of much colder conditions; we ended up using less than a litre. The heater was used maybe three times during the trip and it was never kept alight for more than two hours. The temperatures when at anchor in Kulusuk and in Graah Fjord were generally mild, and with six people generating heat in a small space it was not often necessary.

We had estimated 18kg of gas for cooking. This proved to be relatively accurate: we left the UK with a full 6 litre and 3.9 litre propane bottle. In Iceland, we got another 5 litre bottle. This was exchanged on the return journey, but was still mostly full when we arrived back in Ullapool.

We did a carbon footprint assessment with Carbon Footprint Ltd prior to leaving based on our estimates, and funded the mitigation of 5 tonnes of CO$`_2`$ by contributing to a verified climate project. This will have covered the amounts used, not including the unforeseen airplane journey taken for medical reasons.

## 15. Gearbox Escapades

*Written by Meg Loftus*

A brief technical report of the repairs made to the gearbox. Probably only interesting to those with a techno drive twindisc TMC 60. We had no other significant mechanical or electrical problems. The TMC 60 has a double ended cone which slides on a shaft, allowing it to be pushed by a selecting lever to connect with forwards or reverse gear. The face of the cone is sintered bronze with grooves (when new, ours was basically only bronze) and when it is selected it is connected with a matching female part. When it connects with this the turning engine rotates the cone along a spiral thread which drives it further against the female part. The gearbox then relies on friction between the conical face and the female face to drive the propellor.

If the cable powering the selector lever isn’t properly adjusted, the cone can fail to make enough contact to drive it into the inverse cone. The two faces spinning against each other wears off the grooves and sintering, and the cone loses the necessary friction to engage into gear. For us, this had happened for our forward gear, but the reverse which we use less frequently was still in reasonably good shape. We had to unbolt the gearbox from the engine and prop shaft, and open it up both using the 36mm nut on the engine output shaft and the central divide. It is important that the output shaft nut is taken off first as we didn’t do that and we suspect that is why ours leaked afterward.

We then removed the cone, flipped it round and reassembled. The cone sat on loose bearings and could be taken apart by hand - no bearing pullers required. We resealed the box with silicone sealant but unfortunately the flexible output shaft seal had been damaged so it leaked quite a lot for the rest of the voyage. This was collected in a Tupperware but thankfully we had lots of clean ATF so we didn’t have to recycle. Due to the wear mismatch of the reverse cone and the forward female part, we suspected the gearbox would not last that long and did 2 full oil changes, one before we left Graah Fjord and one in Iceland. Indeed it first began to show signs of slipping when we motored from Ullapool pontoon to Kingfishers winter mooring - survived just long enough!

All in all the actual repair took about one afternoon, but several days were lost researching whether it was possible, if we could get a new one and then latterly trying to find a 36mm socket. Now properly equipped we would probably have only lost a day fixing it, but it was quite a rogue repair to undertake.

## 16. Notes on Future Expeditions

*Written by Will Jones*

With more time, we would have liked to explore the area above *William’s Step* - scrambling up our descent on the left of the crag would provide access to the upper basin. There is a non-technical peak to the northeast (rock and snow). To the west there are various access points to more technical terrain. To the north is a rock ridge that leads onto a glacier with more climbing potential.

We had wanted to try a route on the same wall as *Les Droites*, which we ran out of time to do. Hakkefjeld, northeast of the anchorage, remains unclimbed but we spectulate it could plausibly be done as a multi-day ridge with proper time to scout and assess. An attempt at the true summit of *Phoebe Rose Ridge* would have been satisfying to try.

Attempting *3 Cime* would involve extensive scouting for a feasible descent, but could be the focus of a future expedition - the SAC expedition tried to climb one of the towers, finding loose rock, but there looked to be other interesting options.

To anyone interested in the area, please reach out and we will be able to provide more detailed photos and information on the area.
